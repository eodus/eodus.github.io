## Output

Does generative AI make our work better?

The strongest answer is: generative AI can make work substantially better, but the improvement is conditional, unevenly distributed, and much easier to demonstrate on individual tasks than across entire organizations.

AI can help people produce more, learn faster, and solve problems they could not previously tackle. It can also make people less accurate, weaken expertise, flood organizations with low-value output, and shift effort from creating work to checking it. Both outcomes can occur in the same organization, the same job, and even the same person.

The central question is therefore not simply whether AI improves productivity. It is when AI improves the quality, value, and sustainability of work—and when it merely makes producing output cheaper and faster.

To answer that, we need to distinguish three things:

Task performance: Can someone complete a particular task faster or more accurately?

Professional performance: Does the person or team deliver better work across the full workflow?

Organizational performance: Does the organization create more value after accounting for verification, coordination, errors, training, and other costs?

Evidence of improvement at the first level does not automatically establish improvement at the other two.

1. The strongest case for improvement

The optimistic case is not that AI is a tireless employee that can do everything. It is that AI makes certain forms of expertise, experimentation, and production cheaper and more accessible.

A. AI increases the amount of good work people can do

Many knowledge workers spend substantial time on first drafts, routine correspondence, summarization, coding boilerplate, and searching for information. These activities consume time without always requiring the worker's highest-level judgment.

AI can reduce that burden and leave more time for decisions, relationships, and difficult problems.

The empirical evidence is meaningful:

Customer support: 15% more issues resolved per hour

A field study of 5,172 support agents found that AI assistance increased productivity by about 15% on average. Newer and less-skilled agents gained the most, and their output quality improved. 
Oxford Academic

Consulting: better results on suitable tasks

In an experiment with 758 consultants, GPT-4 users working on tasks within the model's capabilities completed 12.2% more tasks, worked 25.1% faster, and produced significantly higher-quality solutions. 
Organization Science

Software development: higher task completion

Three randomized field experiments involving 4,867 developers at Microsoft, Accenture, and another large company found a 26.1% increase in completed tasks with AI coding assistance, on average. The size of the effect varied across experiments. 
Management Science

These findings support a serious claim: in some real work settings, AI increases output without requiring a proportional increase in human labor, and sometimes improves quality at the same time.

The critical distinction is between generating more output and generating more valuable output. The case for improvement is strongest when both happen together.

B. AI expands what individuals can accomplish

Before generative AI, someone who could write well but lacked design skills might struggle to create a polished presentation. A programmer might need help drafting technical documentation. A small business owner might be unable to afford specialized marketing, translation, or data-analysis support.

AI lowers the cost of moving between disciplines.

That produces at least three kinds of benefit:

Capability expansion: People can undertake tasks previously beyond their skills or resources.

Faster experimentation: They can test more ideas, alternatives, and approaches before committing resources.

Greater accessibility: People with limited language proficiency or less experience can obtain support that was previously expensive or difficult to access.

The economic value may exceed the time saved. A worker who saves 20 minutes on an email gains a little efficiency; a worker who can now build a useful prototype, analyze a dataset, or communicate effectively with a new market may create an entirely new source of value.

C. AI can spread expertise rather than merely replace effort

One of the strongest arguments concerns less-experienced workers.

In the customer-support study, the benefits were largest for novice and lower-skilled agents. The researchers found evidence consistent with AI transmitting useful practices from more experienced workers to their colleagues. They also found signs of improved learning and language fluency. 
Oxford Academic
+1

This suggests a different model of technological progress: AI does not simply automate existing expertise; it can make some of that expertise available to more people.

Potential consequences include:

Faster onboarding.

Less dependence on a small number of experts.

Reduced variation in routine work quality.

More opportunities for smaller teams to undertake sophisticated work.

This is especially important where expertise is scarce and demand for services is high.

There is, however, an important caveat: receiving good suggestions is not the same as developing the ability to solve problems independently. Whether AI builds expertise or substitutes for learning depends on how it is used.

D. AI may improve the experience of work

Work quality includes more than the final deliverable. It also includes frustration, cognitive load, autonomy, and the amount of time people must spend on tedious tasks.

In the customer-support study, AI assistance was associated with better customer sentiment and lower employee attrition. In a separate six-month randomized field experiment involving thousands of knowledge workers, access to an AI tool reduced time spent on email, although effects on activities requiring coordination were more limited. 
Oxford Academic
+1

If AI reduces repetitive work and gives people more room for meaningful activities, it can improve working life even when conventional output metrics understate the benefit.

This is a real improvement—but only if saved time becomes something workers value, rather than an excuse to demand more work at the same pay.

2. The strongest case for degradation

The pessimistic case is not that AI is useless. It is that AI can make the production of work cheaper while making the work itself worse, less trustworthy, or less sustainable.

A. AI can make people confidently wrong

Generative AI produces plausible answers, not guaranteed truths. It can fabricate facts, misunderstand a question, miss a crucial constraint, or construct a persuasive argument from faulty assumptions.

The risk is particularly serious because fluent output can look like competent reasoning.

The BCG–Harvard experiment illustrates this sharply. In a business problem-solving task outside GPT-4's demonstrated capability frontier, participants using the model performed 23% worse than those who worked without it. Yet on a different creative task, AI assistance substantially improved performance. 
BCG
+1

This is not simply a story about a bad model. It is a story about the interaction between model limitations and human judgment.

The danger grows when:

The task requires precise factual or causal reasoning.

Errors are difficult to detect.

Users cannot independently evaluate the answer.

The model sounds equally confident when it is right and wrong.

The organization rewards speed more than correctness.

In these settings, AI can automate the appearance of competence.

B. AI can create work that takes longer to finish

Producing a draft quickly does not mean completing a task quickly.

AI-generated work may require extensive checking, correction, integration, testing, and rework. It may also introduce subtle defects that are more expensive to discover later.

A particularly instructive counterexample comes from a 2025 randomized study by the nonprofit research organization METR. Sixteen experienced open-source developers worked on 246 tasks in mature codebases they already knew. With the AI tools tested, their task completion times increased by 19%, despite the developers expecting AI to make them faster. 
METR
+1

The sample was small and specific, so it should not be generalized to all software development or to newer AI systems. Nevertheless, it establishes an important possibility: AI can make a task feel easier while making it take longer.

The mechanism is straightforward:

AI generates a proposed solution.

The worker must understand and evaluate it.

The solution contains errors, incompatibilities, or unnecessary complexity.

The worker spends time debugging or integrating it.

The net productivity gain disappears—or becomes a loss.

This is especially plausible in unfamiliar, highly interconnected, or reliability-sensitive work.

C. AI can weaken the skills that make people effective

A worker who delegates drafting, analysis, coding, or problem-solving to AI may finish more tasks today but develop less capability to perform those tasks tomorrow.

The risk is a form of cognitive offloading: people stop practicing skills because a tool performs the immediate task for them.

Possible long-term consequences include:

Weaker independent reasoning and problem diagnosis.

Reduced ability to identify subtle errors.

Less familiarity with the foundations of one's profession.

Greater dependence on systems that may be unavailable or unsuitable.

Difficulty developing junior staff into future experts.

These are credible risks, but the evidence needs careful interpretation. Short-term productivity experiments do not establish that AI necessarily erodes skills over time. Conversely, faster completion does not establish that learning has been preserved.

The key question is whether people use AI to extend their own competence or bypass the process of acquiring it.

D. AI can flood organizations with low-value output

When generating a report, presentation, email, or marketing campaign becomes cheap, the volume of material can increase dramatically.

But the capacity to read, evaluate, respond to, and act on that material does not necessarily increase.

This can create an organizational paradox:

More reports, but less time to read them.

More proposals, but more difficulty identifying the good ones.

More messages, but less attention per message.

More code, but a larger testing and maintenance burden.

More content, but less differentiation and trust.

AI can therefore reduce the cost of production while increasing the cost of coordination and selection.

A business may report that each employee produces more documents while becoming no better at making decisions. If everyone can produce ten proposals instead of two, the organization may simply have five times as much material to evaluate.

E. AI can redistribute benefits and risks unfairly

Even when total output increases, not everyone necessarily benefits.

Organizations may capture efficiency gains while workers experience intensified workloads, reduced autonomy, or uncertainty about their jobs. Junior employees may lose access to the routine assignments through which they traditionally acquire professional skills. Workers who are less able to use AI effectively may fall behind those with better tools, training, or managerial support.

The International Labour Organization's June 2026 review concludes that productivity gains are real but uneven, and that worker-reported time savings have not yet consistently translated into higher measured output, earnings, or employment. It highlights inequality, early-career opportunities, worker autonomy, and job quality as important concerns. 
International Labour Organization

This leads to a fundamental distinction:

AI can improve the productivity of a production system without improving the working lives of the people inside it.

A system that produces more by transferring effort, risk, and insecurity to workers may be more efficient in a narrow economic sense, but it is not necessarily better work.

3. What distinguishes improvement from degradation?

The two cases are not mutually exclusive. AI's effects depend on the task, the worker, the organization, and the way success is measured.

The most useful framework is to examine six conditions.

1. Task–model fit

Improvement more likely

Tasks have clear goals, recognizable patterns, and outputs that can be evaluated. AI is demonstrably capable of handling the relevant work.

Degradation more likely

Tasks depend on subtle context, novel causal reasoning, precise facts, or capabilities that have not been validated.

2. Ability to verify the output

Improvement more likely

A person or process can test results cheaply and reliably, using source documents, automated tests, independent review, or objective criteria.

Degradation more likely

Errors are hard to notice, expensive to reverse, or likely to remain hidden until they cause harm.

3. Human expertise

Improvement more likely

AI fills a genuine skills gap, provides useful scaffolding, or gives experts more time for high-value judgment.

Degradation more likely

Users lack the expertise to evaluate results or gradually lose the ability to perform the underlying work themselves.

4. Workflow integration

Improvement more likely

AI fits into existing systems, reduces handoffs, and eliminates unnecessary work.

Degradation more likely

AI adds another tool, creates rework, or produces outputs that other people must spend time interpreting and correcting.

5. Incentives and measurement

Improvement more likely

Success is measured by useful output, quality, customer outcomes, and total cost.

Degradation more likely

Management rewards volume, speed, or apparent busyness while ignoring defects, rework, and downstream costs.

6. Long-term learning and distribution

Improvement more likely

Workers gain capabilities, retain meaningful autonomy, and share in the benefits.

Degradation more likely

Expertise atrophies, entry-level learning opportunities disappear, or efficiency gains translate primarily into intensified work.

These conditions explain why general claims such as "AI makes knowledge workers 20% more productive" are often misleading. The effect depends on which tasks are included, what quality standards apply, and whether the result survives the rest of the workflow.

4. What does the evidence actually establish?

The strongest evidence comes from studies that compare AI-assisted work with a credible counterfactual: what the same type of work would have looked like without AI.

It is useful to separate three levels of evidence.

Level 1: Controlled task experiments

These are particularly good at identifying whether AI helps with a specific task under specified conditions.

For example, the BCG–Harvard experiment found both sides of the story:

Within the model's demonstrated capability frontier, participants completed more work, more quickly, and with higher quality.

Outside that frontier, participants' performance on a business problem-solving task fell by 23% relative to the control group. 
BCG
+1

The lesson is not that AI is good at creative work and bad at analytical work in general. Rather, the relevant capability boundary can cut across conventional categories: some analytical tasks are suitable, while apparently similar tasks are not.

Level 2: Real-world workplace experiments

These test whether benefits survive contact with actual work.

The customer-support and software-development studies are especially informative because they measure work completed in real organizations rather than relying solely on hypothetical tasks.

But even these results need context. The support study measured resolved issues per hour in one company's workflow; the developer experiments measured completed tasks under particular organizational and technical conditions. Neither number should be treated as a universal productivity multiplier.

The METR result reinforces the point: an intervention that helps developers in one setting can slow experienced developers working in familiar, mature codebases in another. 
Management Science
+1

Level 3: Organization-wide and economy-wide outcomes

This is where the case becomes more complicated.

A May 2025 field experiment involving 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers found that employees using an integrated generative AI tool spent less time on email, but researchers did not detect corresponding shifts in the quantity or composition of their work during the experiment. 
NBER

That finding illustrates the gap between saving time and creating additional value.

An organization might use the freed time to:

Produce more and better work.

Reduce overtime or improve work–life balance.

Serve more customers.

Spend more time on complex problems.

Or simply fill the time with additional low-value tasks.

Only the first four represent meaningful benefits, and they are not interchangeable.

The ILO's 2026 review similarly finds that task-level gains have not yet translated consistently into measurable improvements in broader economic outcomes. 
International Labour Organization

This does not prove that AI has no aggregate productivity benefit. Organizational change takes time, and conventional productivity statistics may miss improvements in service quality or product variety. It does mean that evidence of individual time savings is not sufficient evidence of economy-wide transformation.

The key is to measure the whole process, not just the AI-assisted step.

A practical pilot could randomly assign comparable tasks or teams to AI-assisted and standard workflows, define quality criteria in advance, track all time spent on the task, and independently assess a sample of outputs. Where possible, the evaluation should distinguish between:

AI used as a substitute for human effort.

AI used as an assistant that supports human judgment.

AI used as a catalyst for redesigning the workflow itself.

The third option may offer the largest gains, but it also requires the most organizational change.

6. The deeper question: What does "better work" mean?

There is a final complication. Productivity, quality, and human flourishing are related, but they are not the same.

Imagine two organizations that introduce AI.

Organization A produces 30% more reports, but employees have less time to think, customers receive more generic advice, and junior workers learn less.

Organization B produces 10% more useful work, has fewer errors, gives employees more time for difficult problems, and improves its training practices.

If the only measure is output volume, A wins. If the goal is sustainable professional performance, B may be substantially better.

This is why the debate cannot be settled by productivity statistics alone. We must also ask who defines quality, who receives the benefits, who bears the risks, and what forms of human capability we want work to develop.

The most optimistic view is that AI can make expertise more accessible, reduce drudgery, and expand the range of problems people can solve.

The most pessimistic view is that it can commoditize output, weaken expertise, intensify work, and create a system that produces more while understanding less.

Both are plausible. The distinction lies in how AI is deployed and what the organization values.

Conclusion

The evidence supports three conclusions.

1. Generative AI genuinely improves performance on many tasks. Controlled experiments and workplace studies demonstrate real gains in speed, output, and sometimes quality, with especially substantial benefits for some less-experienced workers.

2. Those gains are not universal or automatically sustainable. AI can reduce performance when its capabilities do not fit the task, when users cannot verify its output, or when review and integration costs overwhelm the time saved. Longer-term effects on skills, job quality, and organizational performance remain less settled.

3. The decisive factor is not AI adoption alone, but the quality of the human–AI system. Good task selection, verification, learning, workflow design, and fair incentives determine whether technical capability becomes genuine improvement.

The strongest overall position is neither that AI inevitably makes work better nor that it inevitably degrades it. It is that AI increases the range of possible outcomes—and makes the quality of our decisions about work more consequential.

The test of successful AI adoption is not whether people use AI, or even whether they work faster. It is whether they consistently produce better outcomes, at an acceptable total cost, while preserving the human capabilities and working conditions on which future performance depends.

That is the standard against which claims about AI and the future of work should be judged.
