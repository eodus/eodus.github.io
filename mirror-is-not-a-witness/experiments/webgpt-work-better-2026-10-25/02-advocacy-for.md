## Output

The strongest evidence-based case that generative AI makes our work better

The strongest defensible argument is not that generative AI makes everyone more productive at every task. It is that generative AI can improve the quality, speed, and accessibility of knowledge work by lowering the cost of producing, testing, and refining ideas—and that these gains can translate into better organizational outcomes when work is redesigned around the technology.

This is a substantial claim, but a conditional one. The evidence is strongest for particular tasks, especially writing, coding, information synthesis, and structured problem-solving. It is less conclusive about long-term organizational productivity, innovation, employment, and the quality of work as a whole.

To make the case rigorously, we need to distinguish four things:

Capability: Can AI help someone perform a task better?

Causal impact: Does using AI actually improve performance compared with a credible alternative?

Net benefit: Do the gains outweigh errors, verification, rework, and implementation costs?

Organizational impact: Do individual improvements translate into better products, services, decisions, and outcomes?

The evidence becomes more persuasive when it survives all four tests.

1. The core empirical case

Several influential experiments and field studies provide a strong foundation. They do not prove that every AI deployment works, but they establish that AI assistance can produce measurable improvements in real work.

 
1. Writing faster without sacrificing quality

Noy & Zhang · Science, 2023

In a preregistered randomized experiment involving 453 college-educated professionals, access to ChatGPT reduced the time spent on professional writing tasks by 40% and increased the quality of the output by 18%. Participants included people undertaking tasks such as writing workplace communications and analytical documents. 
PubMed
+1

Why it matters: This is evidence of simultaneous improvement in speed and quality, not merely faster production of inferior work.

Caveat: The tasks were bounded experiments, not fully embedded in complex organizations; factual accuracy and deep company-specific context were not comprehensively tested.

 
2. Improving performance in real workplaces

Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond · Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025

A study of more than 5,000 customer-support agents found that access to a generative AI assistant increased the number of customer issues resolved per hour by approximately 15%. Less experienced and lower-skilled workers benefited most, while the most experienced workers gained little in some measures and saw small declines in certain quality measures. The researchers also found evidence of learning and improved customer interactions. 
Oxford Academic
+1

Why it matters: The improvement appeared in operational data from a real business, not just a laboratory task. It also suggests AI can spread useful practices from experienced workers to newer colleagues.

Caveat: Customer support is a relatively structured setting, and the size of the effect cannot simply be extrapolated to every occupation.

 
3. Extending what skilled professionals can accomplish

Dell’Acqua and colleagues · Organization Science, 2026

In a preregistered experiment with 758 knowledge workers performing realistic consulting tasks, AI users completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, and produced higher-quality solutions on 18 tasks within the model's effective capability range. However, on a complex managerial problem outside that range, AI users were 19 percentage points less likely to produce a correct solution. 
Organization Science
+1

Why it matters: The study supports a strong but conditional argument: AI can expand the effective capabilities of professionals, including on analytical and creative work.

Caveat: The same experiment directly demonstrates that AI can make performance worse when the task exceeds its capabilities.

 
4. Recovering time from routine knowledge work

Dillon and colleagues · field experiment across 66 firms, 2025

In a six-month randomized field experiment involving 7,137 knowledge workers, access to an integrated AI assistant reduced time spent on email among frequent users. The researchers reported about two fewer hours per week in the second half of the experiment among those who used the tool. Yet they did not detect corresponding changes in the overall quantity or composition of work. 
NBER
+1

Why it matters: AI can reduce the cost of routine work across many organizations.

Caveat: Time saved is an intermediate outcome. The study does not establish that the recovered hours consistently became better decisions, greater innovation, or higher organizational output.

Taken together, these findings are unusually persuasive because they include randomized experiments, real workplace data, independent assessments of output quality, and a large cross-firm field experiment. The results are not uniform, but the positive effects are too substantial and recurrent to dismiss as hype alone.

2. Why generative AI can improve work: the causal mechanisms

The most convincing explanation is not that AI is simply an automated employee. It changes the economics of several activities that knowledge workers perform.

Mechanism 1: Lowering the cost of a first draft

Generating a plausible initial email, report outline, explanation, or code snippet is often much cheaper than starting from a blank page. Workers can spend less time on initial production and more time evaluating, correcting, and improving the result.

Predicted effect: Shorter completion times, more iterations, and higher quality when review is effective.

Evidence: The writing experiment found both time and quality gains. 
PubMed
+1

Mechanism 2: Making expertise more accessible

An AI assistant can supply explanations, examples, suggested responses, and procedural guidance that would otherwise require asking a colleague or consulting documentation. It can make some tacit or scattered knowledge available at the moment of need.

Predicted effect: Less time spent searching, fewer avoidable mistakes, and faster development of less experienced workers.

Evidence: The customer-support study found particularly large gains among less experienced workers and evidence consistent with learning. 
Oxford Academic
+1

Mechanism 3: Expanding the set of tasks one person can handle

AI can help a specialist work outside their strongest skill area: a technical expert can explore customer implications, a writer can structure an analysis, or an analyst can explain findings to a nontechnical audience.

Predicted effect: Broader contributions, fewer handoffs, and better integration of technical, commercial, and communication skills.

Evidence: An experiment at Procter & Gamble found that AI-assisted individuals performed as well as teams without AI on product-innovation challenges, and their proposals better integrated technical and commercial perspectives. 
Organization Science
+1

Important distinction: This does not mean AI replaces the trust, accountability, negotiation, or complementary expertise that real teams provide.

Mechanism 4: Increasing the number of ideas worth considering

AI can rapidly generate alternative approaches, counterarguments, examples, and possible explanations. This reduces the cost of exploring an initial set of options.

Predicted effect: Better brainstorming, more alternatives, and potentially stronger solutions after human evaluation.

Evidence: Research on creative writing found that AI-generated ideas improved the average assessed creativity and writing quality of individual stories, particularly for less creative writers. But AI-assisted stories also became more similar to one another. 
Science Advances

Important distinction: More ideas are useful only if they broaden exploration rather than anchor everyone on the same suggestions.

Mechanism 5: Reducing the cost of routine cognitive administration

Summarizing, restructuring, drafting, and transforming information between formats are common but time-consuming parts of office work. Automating portions of them can free attention for tasks requiring judgment, relationships, and original thinking.

Predicted effect: Less time on administrative work and potentially more time for higher-value activities.

Evidence: The six-month, multi-firm experiment found reduced email time among frequent AI users. 
NBER
+1

Important distinction: Whether freed time actually moves to more valuable work depends on incentives, workload, and organizational design.

These mechanisms share an underlying principle: AI lowers the cost of producing a candidate answer, while human judgment can determine whether that answer is useful. The strongest outcomes occur when the cost reduction is large and the result can be evaluated reliably.

3. Why the gains could be larger than the studies currently show

The case for AI is not limited to completing existing tasks more quickly. There are plausible pathways to improving the nature of work itself.

A. More iteration within the same time budget

If producing a first draft becomes cheaper, a worker can compare more alternatives, test more hypotheses, or revise a proposal more thoroughly.

For example, a product manager who previously had time to develop one proposal might use AI to generate three approaches, compare their risks, and test assumptions before committing.

This is a credible mechanism, but its final step is a hypothesis to test, not an automatic consequence of using AI.

B. A higher performance floor

AI assistance may help people who struggle with a task reach an acceptable standard more consistently. The customer-support and writing experiments provide evidence for this equalizing effect. 
PubMed
+1

This could improve organizations in several ways:

Fewer routine tasks requiring escalation.

Less dependence on a small number of highly experienced employees.

Faster onboarding and more consistent service.

More opportunities for people to contribute outside their original specialties.

However, raising the performance floor is not the same as raising the ceiling. If AI makes everyone's output more uniform, it could also reduce differentiation and novel thinking.

C. Better access to knowledge and capabilities

A useful way to think about generative AI is as an interface to capabilities that were previously expensive to access.

Someone who cannot write polished prose can obtain a strong draft. Someone unfamiliar with a programming library can get a starting example. Someone preparing a presentation can explore how to explain a complex topic to different audiences.

The benefit is greatest when the worker has enough knowledge to recognize what good output looks like but lacks the time, fluency, or specialized skill to produce it unaided.

D. Better human collaboration, not just individual output

AI may also help bridge functional boundaries. In the Procter & Gamble experiment, AI-assisted individuals matched the performance of unaided teams on product-innovation challenges and produced more balanced proposals across technical and commercial considerations. 
Organization Science

This suggests that AI can sometimes supply part of the informational breadth that teams achieve by combining different specialists.

But it would be a mistake to conclude that AI makes teams unnecessary. Teams also provide accountability, challenge assumptions, coordinate actions, and build shared commitment—outcomes not captured by a single task-performance score.

4. The strongest limitations and counterarguments

An evidence-based case should not explain away negative findings. It should identify exactly where the argument breaks down.

 

1. AI can produce convincing but incorrect answers

Generative models can make factual errors, misread context, invent details, or offer plausible reasoning that does not establish the correctness of a conclusion. Fluency can make these errors harder to spot.

The consulting experiment is especially important here: AI improved performance on tasks within its capability range but made users less likely to solve a task outside that range correctly. 
Organization Science
+1

What this weakens: The claim that AI is a general-purpose improvement to professional judgment. The benefit depends on the task and the ability to verify results.

 

2. Individual improvement can reduce collective originality

AI can help an individual create a better story, proposal, or design while making the output more similar to everyone else's.

Research on AI-assisted storytelling found exactly this tension: individual stories improved on average, but the stories became more alike. 
Science Advances

What this weakens: The claim that better individual output necessarily produces more innovation at the organizational or societal level.

 

3. Time savings may not become productive gains

A worker might save an hour drafting emails but spend that hour on additional low-value tasks, more meetings, or reworking flawed AI output.

The cross-firm field experiment found reductions in email time but no detectable changes in the quantity or composition of tasks overall. 
NBER
+1

What this weakens: Claims that individual time savings automatically translate into higher organizational productivity or improved working lives.

 

4. AI can help novices more than experts

The customer-support evidence suggests that less experienced workers often gain more, while experienced workers may see smaller gains or some quality deterioration. 
Oxford Academic
+1

Experts may already work efficiently, have strong domain knowledge, or recognize when generic suggestions are inappropriate.

What this weakens: The idea that the same tool and workflow will yield comparable returns for everyone.

 

5. Verification and rework can erase the gains

A draft that takes seconds to generate may require substantial time to check. The more consequential the decision, the more expensive a hidden error can be.

The true economic benefit therefore depends on the full workflow—not the time it takes the model to generate its answer.

What this weakens: Productivity claims based on output speed alone, without accounting for correction, review, and downstream failure.

The evidence supports targeted adoption and measurement, rather than assuming that deploying a tool will improve performance.

A strong evaluation should compare workers or teams using AI with a credible control group doing the same work without it.

A practical design would randomly assign comparable workers to AI-assisted and control conditions, measure baseline performance, and assess outcomes over enough time to distinguish lasting gains from novelty effects. Where randomization is infeasible, a carefully designed phased rollout with a credible comparison group is preferable to simply comparing enthusiastic AI users with nonusers.

The analysis should also be broken down by task type, worker experience, and risk level. An average treatment effect can conceal a substantial benefit for one group and harm for another.

7. The final argument

The strongest case for generative AI rests on three propositions.

First, generative AI can causally improve task performance. Randomized experiments show that people can complete certain writing, customer-support, creative, and consulting tasks faster and, in many cases, better. The evidence includes both controlled studies and real-world deployments. 
PubMed
+2

Second, the improvements have plausible and measurable mechanisms. AI lowers the cost of producing a first draft, makes some expertise more accessible, enables more iterations, and helps people operate across skill boundaries. These are concrete pathways to better work, not merely speculative claims about artificial intelligence.

Third, the gains are conditional, and this is precisely why the case should be taken seriously. AI's effectiveness depends on the task, the worker, the quality of the system, and the ability to detect errors. Organizational benefits also depend on whether time savings translate into higher-value output rather than more work or hidden rework.

The remaining uncertainty is not whether AI can ever make work better. The evidence already establishes that it can. The more consequential questions are how widely those benefits generalize, how they evolve as the technology and workflows mature, and whether organizations can capture the gains without sacrificing accuracy, expertise, originality, or worker well-being.

The strongest defensible conclusion is therefore this: generative AI is a genuine productivity and capability-enhancing technology for a meaningful range of knowledge work. Its value is most likely to be realized when it augments human judgment, is deployed on tasks within its demonstrated capabilities, and is evaluated against outcomes that matter—not when adoption itself is treated as proof of improvement.

That is a stronger argument than claiming AI makes all work better: it specifies the mechanisms, identifies where the evidence is strongest, and makes clear what evidence would prove the claim wrong.
